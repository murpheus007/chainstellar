His chest was tight. Behind him, his relatives whispered prayers. Ahead of him stood the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibini_Ukpabi">Ibini Ukpabi shrine, home of the Long Juju oracle</a>. Every step forward was a gamble. In his village, people didn’t talk about what happened inside. But everyone knew. If the oracle condemned you, you were never seen again. Y<a href="https://dailytrust.com/ibini-ukpabi-the-temple-of-judgment-that-serves-as-slave-route/#:~:text=Arochukwu%2C%20apart%20from%20being%20the,machinery%20in%20every%20Igbo%20community.">ou were sold.</a>/p><p>The Aro priests claimed they delivered judgment. But they didn’t. The core of their system was a business, and like all businesses, there was an exchange.<a href="https://ozikoro.com/arochukwu-oracle-the-spiritual-empire-that-controlled-trade-justice-and-war-in-precolonial-igboland/"> You paid to enter. You paid for a chance at freedom. And if “the gods” found you wanting, you paid with your body</a>. The guilty were often blindfolded and led through tunnels beneath the earth, emerging by the river where traders waited. From there, <a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/integrating-arochukwu-into-the-regional-chronological-structure">they were shipped off to Calabar or Bonny, sold for silver and rum.</a>/p><p>And on top of all this, most of the decisions made by the gods in the Ibini Ukpabi shrine were verdicts that were bought. Still, people kept coming, not necessarily because they trusted the system, but because they believed in the power behind it. The oracle was rigged, but it was also real. It gave answers. It gave outcomes. It gave people something to fear and something to believe in. <a href="https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/1974886">By the 1700s, this system stretched across Southeastern Nigeria.</a> The Aro Confederacy had become a spiritual and commercial empire. Its oracles were used to settle disputes, enforce debts, and punish rivals.</p><p>The illusion lasted for centuries. Then, in 1901–1902, the British launched <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Aro_War">the Anglo‑Aro War</a> to dismantle this system. The oracle shrine was destroyed and burnt, the myth was shattered, a<a href="https://www.africarebirth.com/the-anglo-aro-war-how-the-british-used-an-anti-slavery-campaign-to-conquer-part-of-igboland/">nd they installed their own system</a>. But the habit of engaging with powerful, expropriative systems never went away. It simply evolved. Because it wasn’t the gods that were destroyed — it was an economy.</p><p><strong>SCENE 2 — <em>Still History!</em></strong></p><p><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Niger_Company">The Royal Niger Company (RNC)</a>, founded in 1886 from George Goldie’s earlier trading operations, was granted a royal charter giving it the power to administer territories, sign treaties, collect taxes, and operate its own militias, essentially functioning as a private government supported by the British Crown. The company is said to have signed over 400 treaties with local chiefs, <a href="https://africadailynews.net/2025/07/18/british-empires-corruption-and-exploitation-in-nigeria">that tricked them into granting trade monopolies, land rights, and sovereignty to the company</a> in exchange for trivial gifts.</p><p>Once these treaties were secured, the RNC enforced <a href="https://fliphtml5.com/ajfm/mwcd/THE_ECONOMIC_IMPERIALISM_OF_THE_ROYAL_NIGER_COMPANY%2A">an economic monopsony</a>: it purchased palm oil, ivory, and shea butter at suppressed prices, while simultaneously forcing communities to buy imported goods (often gin or rum), and all this was driving wealth outward. Even when chiefs like <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaja_of_Opobo">Jaja of Opobo</a> or <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_William_Koko_Mingi_VIII_of_Nembe">King Koko of Nembe</a> resisted this system, they faced exiles, assassinations, or violent reprisals. <a href="https://www.zikoko.com/citizen/citizen-history-how-king-koko-created-the-white-mans-graveyard-in-bayelsa/">In 1895, King Koko attacked the RNC base in Akassa</a>; the British retaliated by burning Brass and executing punitive violence as a warning to all.</p><p>While the Royal Niger Company extracted from Nigeria, Britain’s working class benefited significantly.<a href="https://www.primebusiness.africa/the-oil-palm-war-in-nigeria-special-report"> Palm oil was essential to the British Industrial Revolution</a>, for things like lubricants, soaps, and steel machines. So basically, by shifting stripping and exploitation abroad, especially to colonies like Nigeria, Britain reduced domestic hardship and unrest, improving the standard of living at home. <a href="https://youtu.be/Ha2SLXYoJsM?si=y6Uz4Ejp59vnU1b9">And this system survived well into the 20th century.</a>/p><p>After the devastation caused by years of colonial and corporate extraction, Nigeria needed a way out. That’s when the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Monetary_Fund">IMF</a> arrived, offering ‘redevelopment loans’ that came with fine print and far-reaching consequences. In July 1986, under pressure from the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigeria_and_the_International_Monetary_Fund">Nigeria adopted a sweeping Structural Adjustment Programme</a>. The underlying rules were clear:</p><ul><li><a href="https://cepr.net/documents/publications/debt_1999_04.htm">Slash public spending on education, health, and subsidies</a>/li><li><a href="https://ricyeboah.com/2016/08/10/the-impact-of-neoliberalism-on-post-colonial-africa-in-the-20th-century">Block national ownership, enabling Western privatization and profit</a>/li><li><a href="https://muse.jhu.edu/article/838367?">Prioritize export industries over local welfare</a>/li><li><a href="https://muse.jhu.edu/article/838367">And ultimately Devalue the naira</a>/li></ul><p><a href="https://punchng.com/imf-strange-pill-to-cure-worsening-economic-crisis">Acceptance to these terms was mandatory to unlock IMF loans.</a>/p><p><strong>SCENE 3 — <em>Now</em></strong></p><p>Dera walked with his military-themed Crocs down the red marbled path toward his uncle’s Cybertruck. His chest was gleefully beating. Behind him, his uncle walked towards the driver’s seat of the Cybertruck, whispering a song. Ahead of them stood the tall, heavy gate that was opened by the gateman in the building, and Dera and his uncle drove out. The basics such as food, shelter, and clothing were all they could ever desire, and even the vanities of those times still circled around that same axis: land, foodstuffs, cowries, farm tools, and livestock. The products used back then directly supported the existence and maintenance of those basics.</p><p>Not much has changed since then. Even the caste of society responsible for building beyond the basics, like reliable infrastructure, remain innately driven by baseline survival needs. This is why many formal systems extracted value without building durable local capacity. The result wasn’t just poverty, it was a deep-rooted orientation toward improvisation culture. The mismatch between product design intent and Nigerian reality explains why this culture persists: adaptive responses to scarcity, cost constraints, and institutional failure, shaped by deeper systemic challenges in infrastructure, governance, and public trust.</p><p>Take <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerosene">kerosene</a>, for instance, whether imported or locally refined, its intended use has always been fuel for cooking stoves and lamps. But in rural communities, kerosene became a go-to multipurpose product. Beyond its role in lighting or cooking, it has been used as <a href="https://evergreensolution.co/safer-alternatives-for-kerosene-in-industrial-cleaning/">a solvent for cleaning</a>, a <a href="https://www.rrh.org.au/journal/article/1858/">base for homemade balms, and even as a treatment for wounds and skin irritations</a>. These unofficial uses were driven by a combination of factors: economic scarcity that limited access to proper medical supplies; mistrust toward expensive or inaccessible healthcare systems; and the natural tendency to improvise with whatever was available at home.</p><p>Plastic containers, originally packaging for cooking oil, paint, or industrial products, have also been widely repurposed. In homes across Nigeria, especially in lower-income or rural areas, <a href="https://opinion.premiumtimesng.com/2018/07/10/dangers-of-food-and-water-in-plastic-containers-by-kayode-ojewale">these containers are used to store food, fetch water, or transport goods on farms</a>. Their durability made them excellent substitutes for costlier, purpose-built household tools. Again, the drivers were familiar, the high cost of formal storage items, limited product availability, and a long-standing culture of reuse where nothing is considered waste if it can still serve a function.</p><p>Similarly, empty tomato paste cans, like those from Gino, have taken on entirely new lives after their original contents are consumed. <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331121749_The_tomato_paste_tin_can_An_African_journey_Burkina_Faso">These cans are frequently used as measuring cups in local markets, small storage containers, or even improvised cooking pots and stoves for delicacies like moi moi.</a> The need to stretch resources, coupled with the lack of standardized tools in informal market settings, ensured their widespread adoption.</p><p>The arrival of digital banking brought new examples. USSD codes like *737# or *894#,<a href="https://www.itnewsafrica.com/2023/11/unlocking-financial-inclusion-ussd-technology-in-african-mobile-money"> initially launched to enable simple banking without internet access and smartphones</a>, quickly became the foundation of financial access for millions, even those with both internet access and smartphones. Their simplicity, affordability (no data costs), and reliability in low-connectivity environments turned them into <a href="https://african.business/2017/01/finance-services/nigeria-ussd-tech-powers-mobile-banking-revolution">Nigeria’s default financial rail</a>. Long before mobile apps gained traction, <a href="https://marketingedge.com.ng/how-customers-increased-trust-in-firstbanks-894-ussd-banking-can-boost-the-financial-inclusion-drive">USSD filled the gap in a trust-starved system</a>, especially for micro-transfers, airtime purchases, and peer-to-peer transactions.</p><p>Finally, one of the most iconic examples of improvisation in Nigeria is the use of small and large generator sets. Originally designed as <em>backup</em> power solutions — keyword: <strong>backup</strong> — they have become the primary electricity source for entire neighborhoods, especially in cities where power supply is unreliable (which is basically every city in Nigeria). The stats are there, top two globally in adoption, billions in yearly volume, wallets everywhere. But scratch the surface, and you’ll find that much of this adoption is not really rooted in deep, daily-life utility. Crypto’s earliest arrival here was carried on the backs of high-yield promises and <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponzi_scheme">Ponzi</a>-shaped networks, with <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MMM_(Ponzi_scheme_company)">MMM</a>, <a href="https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/forsage-founders-indicted-340m-defi-crypto-scheme">Forsage</a>, and <a href="https://www.icirnigeria.org/anxiety-mixed-reactions-trail-suspected-racksterli-ponzi-scheme/">Racksterli</a>, and, more recently, <a href="https://punchng.com/recovery-ongoing-in-n1-3tn-cbex-ponzi-collapse-efcc/">CBEX</a>, as well as countless <a href="https://www.nairaland.com/5655641/telegram-investment-grouping">Telegram “investment groups,</a>” as clear examples. That DNA and behaviour hasn’t entirely faded. Even now, a significant slice of activity revolves around short-term speculation like buying dips, flipping pumps aka <a href="https://equalsmoney.com/financial-glossary/degen-trading">degen trading</a>, “<a href="https://www.nigeriabitcoincommunity.com/kaito-ai/#:~:text=Yapping%20simply%20means%20tweeting%20about,not%20every%20tweet%20earns%20points.">yapping</a>” and chasing <a href="https://bridgeafricatech.com/dollar-arbitrage-in-nigeria/">arbitrage between naira and dollar rates.</a>/p><p>Stablecoins like USDT and USDC dominate our flows, accounting for roughly 40–43% of Sub-Saharan Africa’s crypto inflows. But here’s the question: is that <em>crypto</em> adoption, or just a more efficient way to hold dollars? If all you’re doing is using your <a href="https://phantom.com/">Phantom wallet</a> or <a href="https://metamask.io/">Metamask</a> as a digital <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_de_change">forex bureau</a>, you’re not embedded in the ecosystem, you’re sidestepping your national currency. And this is not a bad thing, hedging against inflation is a legitimate use case. But it’s a shallow kind of product-market fit (PMF). It means adoption is still reactive, tied to naira volatility, rather than proactive, where users keep coming back because the system gives them consistent, irreplaceable value in normal times.</p><p><strong>SCENE 2 — <em>The Policy Rollercoaster and Trust Deficit</em></strong></p><p>Cryptocurrency’s history in Nigeria has been a long dance with uncertainty.<a href="https://www.cbn.gov.ng/out/2021/ccd/volume%203%20number%202%20cbn%20update%20february%202021.pdf"> In 2021, the Central Bank of Nigeria instructed banks to stop enabling crypto transactions</a>, a move that pushed large swathes of activity underground into P2P markets, where innovation thrived but risks multiplied.
